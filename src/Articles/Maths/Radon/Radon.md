<!--The CT scanner-->

The 3D image/CT volume of the radiology report generated by the CT machine is a discrete array of cross-sectional approximant slices of the body made less than 1mm apart from each other. The machine has a rotating ring structure they call **gantry**. It has an x-ray tube which emits divergent x-rays with known initial intensity. The x-rays get attenuated - absorbed - as they pass through different tissues and the final intensity is measured by the detector ring at the other side of the ring assembly. The detector array can have varying width in the order of hundreds, and number of rows in the order of tens. The fact that body tissue abrorbs the x-ray in function of pointwise density (and light intensity) is precisely what accounts for the existence of the method, the ability to create the approximant map, while it is also the reason behind its precautions.

The gantry completes a full cycle around the body at some z-axis position and then offsets forward in some function of the number of rows of the detectors. It would be convenient to think of it as each row should account for exactly one cross-sectional reconstruction/approximant, and then the gantry should offset exactly by the full-height of the rows combined along the z-axis but that may not be the case. One cross-section may be the result of measurements of complete cycles of just one, or multiple rows of detectors as the gantry moves forward.

<!--Radon transform-->

For convenience, think of each cross section as a rectangle. The information collected for one ray is:
- Angle/slope
- Line equation constant $s$ which accounts for the y-axis displacement of the line compared to the origin $(0,0)$

After completion of a full $360^{\circ}$ cycle, the x-ray information of parallel x-ray measurements are grouped by their angle. Precisely, we aim for creating the functions with constant angles $\phi$ which describe the total accumulated attenuation/absorbtion in function of line equation constant $s$:

$$
f_{\phi}(s)=\Delta I
$$

Naively, if the initial x-ray intensity at time of emission was $I_0$, and the final intensity was $I_f$:

$$
f_{\phi}(s)=\Delta I=I_0 - I_f
$$

The problem with this is that it is not an empirically correct way of measuring instrinsic constant pointwise attenuation of body tissue. The above assumes constant pointwise absorbtion of x-ray, but empirical evidence says pointwise absorption is in fact a **constant rate/percentage** of incoming intensity at the point. Let $\mu_{\phi, s}(t)$ denote the intrinsic attenuation constant of the tissue along the line at angle $\phi$ with constant $s$ at position $t$. Then the above would imply:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mu_{\phi, s}(t) dt = I_0 - I_f\\
\implies I_f + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mu_{\phi, s}(t) dt = I_0
$$

Which exactly means that all the attenuation along the same line is completely insensitive of/is not a function of the x-ray intensity passing through, that is, regardless of the initial intensity $I_0$ of the x-ray, the final intensity $I_f$ after passing through the body through the exact same line should decrease by the exact same amount, which is empirically incorrect. Empirical evidencce says pointwise attenuation is stable (constant) when considered as percentage absorbtion of the incoming/remaining x-ray intensity. Therefore change in intensity at point $t_0$ on the line in a small forwards neighborhood can be approximated as:

$$
I(t_0 + h) - I(t_0) = -I(t_0)\cdot\mu_{\phi,s}(t_0 + h)h
$$

Over the line, change of $I$ over any two points can be expressed as the limit of ever refining sum of changes of linear functions whose slopes are derived from $-\mu\cdot I$, that is, $I$ is the integral function and $-\mu\cdot I$ is its derivative $I'$:

$$
-\mu_{\phi, s}(t_0) \cdot I(t_0)=\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{I(t_0 + h) - I(t_0)}{h}=\frac{d}{dt} I(t)\bigg|_{t=t_0}
$$

However, the cross-sectional 2D image shall be represent by just function $\mu$, because that is the instrinsic characteristic of the body. Rearrange the above equation, so that $\mu$ is separated from function $I$:

$$
-\mu_{\phi, s}(t)=\frac{I^{'}(t)}{I(t)}
$$

Integrate both sides:

$$
-\int \mu_{\phi,s}(t) dt = \ln(|I(t)|) 
$$

Between initial intensity $I_0$ and final intensity $I_f$:

$$
-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mu_{\phi,s}(t) dt = \ln(|I_f|) - \ln(|I_0|)\\
\implies \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mu_{\phi,s}(t) dt = \ln(|I_0|) - \ln(|I_f|)
$$

Which is exactly the Radon transform along the line at angle $\phi$ and shift constant $s$:

$$
R\mu(\phi, s)=\ln(|I_0|) - \ln(|I_f|)
$$

The duty of the CT machine such line integral values along many different angles ($\phi$), and many different constant shifts ($s$), that is, to have enough samples of this function. Eventually the goal is to retrieve function $\mu$ as a two-variable function $\mu(x, y)$ which describes the cross-sectional image. In fact, the above $\mu_{\phi,s}(t)$ function can be expressed as a compound parametrized function:

$$
R\mu(\phi,s)
=
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\mu(s\cos\phi-t\sin\phi,\;
s\sin\phi+t\cos\phi)\,dt=\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mu(x_{\phi, s}(t), y_{\phi,s}(t))\,dt
$$

The slope of a line describes the constant ratio between change in height $\Delta y$ over change in width $\Delta x$ (the constant rate of direct proportionality between two variables). One very natural and straightforward way to express it in terms of angle is the tangent:

$$
\tan(\gamma) = \frac{\sin(\gamma)}{\cos(\gamma)} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}
$$

Due to the identity $-\cot(\phi=\gamma+\frac{\pi}{2})=\tan(\gamma)$ it can also be expressed as:

$$
\tan(\gamma) = \frac{\sin(\gamma)}{\cos(\gamma)} = \frac{-\sin(\phi)}{\cos(\phi)}
$$

So that vector $\vec{n}_{\perp} = (-\sin(\phi), \cos(\phi))$ is a vector parallel to the direction of any line of this slope. The reason for this less intuitive expression is the beauty of the normal vector $\vec{n} =(\cos(\phi), \sin(\phi))$ which is perpendicular to that direction vector. Hence arbitrary point on parallel lines of fixed slope defined by angle $\phi$ with given shift constant $s$ can be expressed as:

$$
\mu_{\phi}(s, t)=s \cdot \vec{n}_{\perp} + t \cdot \vec{n}
$$

That means the $x$ and $y$ coordinate input based re-expression of $\mu(s,t)$ to $\mu(x(s, t), y(s, t))$ can exactly be done as the $x$ and $y$ coordinates have alread been defined above in function of $s$ and $t$:

$$
\mu_{\phi}(s,t)\ \text{can be redefined such that:}\\
\mu_{\phi}(x(s,t), y(s,t))\\
x(s,t)=s\cos\phi-t\sin\phi\\
y(s,t)=s\sin\phi+t\cos\phi
$$

<!--Fourier slice theorem-->

The Fourier slice theorem states that the one dimensional Fourier transform/integral of the Radon transform function, with a fixed angle $\phi$, integrated with respect to the shift variable $s$ equals the $\phi$ angle slice of the 2 dimensional Fourier transform of the original attenuation function $\mu(x, y)$: 

$$
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}\{R\mu_{\phi}(s)\}(\omega)
=
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}^2}\{\mu(x,y)\}
\left(\omega\cos\phi,\omega\sin\phi\right)
$$

Expressed as integrals:

$$

\int_{\mathbb{R}}
R\mu(\phi,s)e^{-i2\pi\omega s}\,ds
=
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}
\mu(x,y)e^{-i2\pi\omega(x\cos\phi+y\sin\phi)}
\,dx\,dy
$$
